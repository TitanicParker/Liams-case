# Liam’s Case

## The Table

Jo found the pencil by sitting on it. She stood, reached behind her and placed it between the two mugs with more ceremony than the achievement deserved. “There.” Sam looked at the pencil. “That was the difficult part.” “It was becoming one.” They had brought the papers into the kitchen because the shed table was still carrying half a bicycle pump, three jars of screws, a tin of washers and a length of timber neither of them could remember deciding to keep. The kitchen table was not clear either, exactly, but it was flat. Sam had printed the first few pages; Jo had the laptop open. Between them sat a medical history neither of them knew. They had been given only one instruction before they began: do not begin with the answer.

“So what are we looking for?” Sam asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

“That’s reassuring.”

“It should be.” Jo looked at the heading again. “Start before Neurology.”

“Why?”

“Because Neurology doesn’t get to decide what was sent to Neurology.”

That seemed fair enough. The first date that mattered was 10 April 2017. The GP record described bilateral foot pain — pain in both feet — alongside an abnormal gait, meaning the way the patient walked. There were mallet toes, callouses and pressure areas, and Podiatry was planned. Podiatry, Jo reminded Sam, was the part of healthcare concerned with feet and lower-limb problems: skin, nails, pressure, mechanics, footwear, structure and function. It was already important that a Podiatry referral existed before anyone had settled what the whole presentation meant. Sam read the entry twice. “Feet. And gait. Already together.”

“Together in the same visit,” Jo said. “Not necessarily together as one thing.”

It was a small distinction, but it stopped them doing something they would have to keep resisting: turning proximity into explanation.

By 8 June, difficulty initiating movement had appeared in the GP record. Neurology was being considered for the gait, the feet and the “muscle tension.” By 23 June there was increased leg tone and brisk reflexes. *Tone* here meant the background resistance or tension felt in muscles when a limb is moved; increased tone can accompany several neurological conditions. *Brisk reflexes* simply meant the tendon reflexes responded more strongly than expected. Alcohol appeared too, but not as a diagnosis: it was described as something the patient used most nights to relax the muscles. Jo wrote in the margin, *Alcohol present. Neuropathy explanation: not yet.*

“You’ve made a rule already,” Sam said.

“I’ve made a warning.”

“That looks suspiciously like a rule.”

She let him write the word beside it, then drew a line through it. On 29 June the referral became urgent. Dexterity and work function were worsening, and an assessment was arranged for Monday, 3 July, at 08:30. The referral asked, in effect, whether the patient was *Parkinsonian* or whether something else was going on. Sam stopped there. “Parkinsonian is one of those words people think they understand until they have to say what it means.” Jo pulled a clean sheet toward her. “Then we don’t make the reader guess either.”

They made themselves a working vocabulary before going any further. It was not a diagnostic glossary and it was not a list of conclusions about this patient. It was simply enough plain English to prevent the technical words from acquiring a false authority of their own. *Parkinsonism*, they wrote, was a clinical pattern rather than one single cause: features such as slowness of movement, stiffness or rigidity, tremor in some people, and characteristic changes in posture or walking. *Parkinsonian* meant resembling or belonging to that pattern. It did not, by itself, mean that every symptom had been explained or that the final cause was known. *Bradykinesia* meant slowness and reduced amplitude of voluntary movement, one of the central features clinicians look for in Parkinsonism.

*Dystonia* was different. It referred to involuntary muscle contractions producing abnormal, sometimes twisting movements or postures. A limb could therefore look *dystonic* without the word itself settling why it was dystonic. *Foot drop* was another distinct idea: difficulty lifting the front of the foot, commonly discussed in relation to weakness or nerve dysfunction. So when a neurologist later wrote that the lower limb looked “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP,” the distinction would matter. It meant the appearance might superficially resemble a dropped foot while the clinician was explicitly interpreting it as something else.

They added the structural terms. *Pes planus* meant flat feet. *Hammertoes* described toes held in a bent deformity. Corns and callouses were thickened areas of skin associated with pressure or friction. These could be entirely genuine physical findings without answering the separate question of what had produced the loading, posture, movement or pain around them.

Then Jo added several words they knew were coming. A *peripheral neuropathy* was a disorder of nerves outside the brain and spinal cord and could produce symptoms such as burning, numbness, pain, altered sensation or weakness, depending on the nerves involved. A *DaTscan* was a nuclear-medicine scan used to look at dopamine-transporter activity in the brain. An abnormal scan can support the presence of a dopaminergic movement disorder, but it does not identify the cause of every symptom in a person’s body. *Levodopa* was a drug the brain can convert into dopamine; Sinemet was one common levodopa-containing medicine. *Procyclidine* was an anticholinergic drug sometimes used to reduce certain Parkinsonian or dystonic symptoms. *Rasagiline* was another Parkinson’s medication, working through a different mechanism. *Pregabalin*, also called Lyrica, was often used for neuropathic pain. *Stalevo* combined levodopa with other drugs intended to prolong or stabilise its effect.

“What about OFF?” Sam asked. Jo wrote that too. In Parkinson’s care, an *OFF* period meant a time when medication benefit had worn down or symptoms had re-emerged before the next dose took effect. So an expression such as *OFF dystonia* did not mean “dystonia that is absent”; it meant dystonia associated with a low-medication or worn-off state. Finally they wrote *PARK2 / PRKN / Parkin*. PRKN was the gene; Parkin was the protein it encoded, and PARK2 was an older disease label often used in discussions of Parkin-related young-onset Parkinsonism. Much later, the record would contain two pathogenic PRKN variants and the phrase *compound heterozygous*: two disease-causing variants, one on each copy of the gene. Sam stared at the page. “That’s a lot to know before breakfast.”

“We don’t have to know it,” Jo said. “We just have to stop pretending the words are explanations.”

That became the point of the sheet. The terms would help them recognise what clinicians were seeing and considering, but the terms were not addresses. A word such as *Parkinsonian*, *neuropathic* or *dystonic* could narrow a question without settling every neighbouring one. Sam went back to the referral. “So what’s actually being handed over?” Jo looked at the notes rather than answering from memory. “Painful feet, abnormal gait, increased tone, movement trouble, toe clawing, worsening dexterity, worsening function — and possible Parkinsonism.”

“That still sounds like a list.”

“It is a list.”

“I thought you were going to tell me what the problem was.”

“I don’t think they know yet. That may be the most accurate thing we can say.”

They made tea. While the kettle boiled, Sam tried to give the referral a cleaner shape. “So: Parkinson’s and a foot problem.” Jo turned from the cupboard. “No.” He looked pleased rather than annoyed. “Good. Why?”

“Because you’ve just put in the separation.”

“I said *and*.”

“Exactly. Look at what was actually sent.”

The feet were painful, the gait abnormal, tone increased, movement initiation difficult, the toes clawing and function deteriorating. Possible Parkinsonism was one of the things to be worked out. Nothing in the referral required the feet to sit outside the movement problem. Sam read it again. “So the feet and the movement are together here.”

“Together on the page, anyway.”

He crossed out *Parkinson’s + foot problem* and wrote underneath: *one unresolved presentation*. Jo did not correct him. The patient attended on 3 July. A CT brain was performed that day. Severe Parkinsonism was suspected or recognised. The work-up began. The exact timing of every bedside observation over the following days was not available. That mattered immediately. There were things Jo and Sam could place inside the admission. They could not always place them on a particular hour or even a particular day. “Annoying,” Sam said.

“Useful.”

“How is not knowing useful?”

“It stops us pretending.” He looked at her.

“Also annoying.”

During the admission the record contained severe young-onset Parkinsonism, marked tone, bradykinesia and a striking gait. Then came the lower-limb description. Sam read it aloud.

“Somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — not foot drop.” He stopped.

“Capital letters?”

“Apparently.”

“Someone wanted that bit clear.”

The same examination recorded pes planus and hammertoes. Sam looked from one line to the other.

“So it looks neurological.”

“And the feet are structurally abnormal.”

“Which one is it?” Jo reached for her mug.

“Why one?”

He looked down again. There was nothing difficult about the words. There was a neurological syndrome. There were structural abnormalities. There was a lower limb behaving in a way the neurologist thought looked dystonic rather than weak. There were painful feet. Two real things could occupy the same body without politely arranging themselves into separate departments. Sam said, “Both can be true.” Jo took a sip of tea.

“Apparently.”

It was the first thing all afternoon that felt like an answer. They did not write a category beside it. On 4 July there were more investigations. On 5 July, more laboratory samples. On 6 July the GP contacted Podiatry. The case was to be prioritised “as Parkinson’s.” Sam frowned.

“So by the sixth they’ve decided the feet are Parkinsonian.”

“No.”

“It literally says as Parkinson’s.”

“For priority.” He looked back at the note.

“Ah.”

“The route is administrative.”

“Not causal.”

“Not necessarily.” He drew two boxes.

*why Podiatry sees him*

and

*why the feet are like this*

He put a line between them, then immediately rubbed it out.

“Too early?”

“Very.”

The rubber left a grey patch on the paper. Sam stared at it.

“Good. We’ve damaged the evidence.”

“You’ve damaged the photocopy.”

“I knew there was a distinction.”

They kept going. By now a peculiar feature of the work had begun to show itself. A thing could be entirely real and still fail to answer the question beside it. Podiatry could be appropriate. That did not tell them why the feet were painful. Structural abnormalities could be genuine. That did not tell them whether movement, tone or posture affected them. Parkinsonism could be obvious. That did not make every other problem secondary. The more facts they acquired, the less willing Jo became to let one of them do another’s job. Sam noticed.

“You keep asking what each thing actually settles.”

“Yes.”

“Is that from the shed?” Jo thought about it.

“Probably.”

He nodded. Then they forgot about the shed again. The first date that changed the atmosphere was 9 July. Not because of something dramatic at the bedside. Because of a time. 16:12. Sam found it first.

“Here.”

Document 04 contained a line referring to results at “transcription finalisation,” added by TM, with a results status timestamped 16:12. Jo moved her chair closer.

“So there’s already a document.”

“There’s some state of it.”

“Same thing.”

“No.” She gave him the look he normally gave her. He laughed.

“Fine. Not the same thing.”

They read the source note carefully. The timestamp proved that a documentary state existed at 16:12 on 9 July. It did not prove that every sentence in the completed Document 04 existed at that moment. It was less satisfying than a clean answer, and much more useful. Sam drew a horizontal line across a fresh page. At the left he wrote:

*3 JUL — admission*

Farther along:

*9 JUL 16:12 — document state*

Then:

*10 JUL — MRI / US*

He stopped. “We don’t know when all the main wording appeared. It could have been before this timestamp or after it.”

“Exactly.” Jo smiled.

“Excellent. I hate the hang of it.”

On 10 July the MRI brain and cervical spine and the abdominal ultrasound were performed. The Podiatry system also showed registration on 10 July. The DaTscan had not yet happened. They had now reached the edge of what they could safely know before the next event. Sam had already read ahead once and been told off. He did it again.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re reading the next page.”

“I’m orienting myself.”

“You’re contaminating yourself.”

“That is a much more impressive accusation.”

Jo held out her hand. He gave her the page. She put it face down.

“Before we go on, tell me what exists.” He sighed theatrically and looked at the timeline.

Sam worked through the state of play aloud. The GP had sent an integrated problem: painful feet, abnormal gait, increased tone, movement difficulty, loss of function and possible Parkinsonism. By 10 July Neurology had recognised severe young-onset Parkinsonism, described a dystonic-looking lower limb that was not foot drop, and recorded genuine structural foot abnormalities. Podiatry was active; MRI and ultrasound had happened; genetic or PARK2 thinking and treatment strategy were developing; and at least one identifiable state of the specialist document existed by 9 July at 16:12.

Jo let him finish before asking the harder half. “And what does not exist yet?”

“The DaTscan. Later treatment response. Later genetics. The 2020 Parkinsonian-foot observation. The 2023 pain material. The complaint.” He looked at the face-down page. “And we do not know whether the final wording about the feet already exists.”

“That one,” Jo said.

Sam wrote it in larger letters.

*we do not know the wording yet*

He looked at it.

“That feels odd.”

“What does?”

“We’ve got the final document, so we know what it eventually says. But if we use all of that now, we stop standing on 10 July.”

“Exactly. We know it. We’re choosing not to import it.”

She tapped the date. 10 July 2017. Sam looked at the line he had drawn. For the first time the table seemed to have acquired a direction of its own. Not left to right exactly. More like before and after had become physical. The pile on Jo’s side contained things that had happened. The pile on Sam’s side contained things that would happen. Between them was a narrow strip of bare wood. “What do we call that?” he asked.

“The middle of the table.”

“You know what I mean.”

Jo did. But she did not answer. Not yet. They turned to the next material. The patient would later say that on 10 or 11 July, during a bedside teaching interaction, he raised a simple complaint. His feet had not been dealt with. Sam read the later account. Then he read the source-status note. Then he read both again.

“So this happened.” Jo shook her head.

“He says it happened.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“No, it isn’t.” He opened his mouth, then closed it.

“Fair.”

There was no contemporaneous retained-core note that expressly recorded the event. Years later, the patient would seek records capable of identifying or reconstructing it. The hospital would say it did not hold responsive data in several of the requested student, ward-round and teaching categories. That later search result did not prove the encounter had not occurred. It did not prove that it had. Jo placed the claim onto the timeline in pencil. Sam noticed.

“Pencil?”

“For a reason.” They bracketed the event:

*10–11 JUL — claimed Protest*

Sam read the substance again.

“You have not dealt with my feet.”

It was strikingly plain. No dopamine argument. No genetics. No neuropathy theory. No movement-disorders vocabulary. No later explanation of what the feet “really” were. Just the practical problem that had brought the patient into care. The feet were still a problem. Sam said, “He doesn’t need to be right about the cause. He could be completely wrong about what is causing it and still be right that the problem he came in with has not been dealt with.” Jo looked at him.

“That seems important.”

He picked up the pencil. For a moment he seemed about to write something large. Instead he wrote one word.

*notice?*

Then added a question mark. They let it sit. The timing now mattered in a way it had not a few minutes before. If the account was accurate, the claim came after much of the work-up had occurred. But before the DaTscan. Before physical discharge had become documentary finality. Before the surviving specialist synthesis had reached its final signed state. And, critically, before they could safely say when several of its consequential phrases had been written. Sam reached for the completed Document 04. Jo did not stop him this time. He found the paragraph. There it was. The leg symptoms, including corns, callouses and burning, “may have been completely separate,” or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy, perhaps related in some respect to significant original alcohol intake. Not clear.

Then the statement that the patient had, in his own mind, “markedly divorced” the feelings of tension in the legs from the corns, callouses and pains in the feet. Sam looked up.

“That’s quite a lot.”

“Yes.”

“Do we know when that paragraph was written?”

“No.”

“Before the complaint?”

“We don’t know.”

“After?”

“We don’t know.”

“Before he left?”

“We don’t know.” He looked irritated now, but not with Jo.

“So we have to keep two things apart.”

“Which two?”

“What the final document says.”

“And?”

“When the final document came to say it.” Jo smiled.

“That’s better.”

He put the page flat on the table. The finished paragraph looked authoritative. It was complete. It had shape. It had possibilities. It had a route. It had a patient-attributed distinction. It had a hypothesis about neuropathy. It had alcohol. It had uncertainty. It had *Not clear*. It looked like a thought that had reached its end. But the timeline underneath it did not yet allow Jo and Sam to assume that the thought had existed in that form at the moment the patient said his feet had not been dealt with. That was new. The sentence and the event were no longer sitting in the same time merely because they appeared in the same history. Sam pushed his chair back.

“Tea?”

“We still have some.” He picked up his mug and looked inside.

“No, we have evidence that tea used to be here.” Jo laughed.

“That’s terrible.”

“It’s accurate.”

They made another pot. When they came back, Jo turned the completed Document 04 face down again.

“Why are you doing that?”

“Because I want one more pass at the moment before it.” Sam sat.

Sam reconstructed the moment in one pass. The patient was still in hospital. Severe Parkinsonism had been recognised; the feet were structurally abnormal; the lower limb looked dystonic; Podiatry was going ahead; imaging had been done, though not the DaTscan; and some state of the specialist document already existed. If the later account of the Protest was accurate, the patient was also saying that the feet still had not been dealt with.

Sam looked at the bare strip of table between the piles.

“So what is unresolved now?”

Jo did not answer immediately. The easy answer was the diagnosis. But Parkinsonism was already strongly present. The easy answer was the feet. But the feet were visible, documented, structurally abnormal and already routed to Podiatry. The problem was somewhere between those answers. Sam got there first.

“How the feet belong to the rest of it.”

Jo nodded. Not what the feet were in isolation. Not whether Parkinsonism was real. Not whether Podiatry had a legitimate job to do. The unresolved thing was the relationship. Sam wrote that down. This time Jo did not change a word. Outside, someone put a bin lid down too hard. The sound came through the kitchen window and made them both look up. Then Sam turned the next page over. The patient was going to leave hospital before the document was finished. Neither of them knew that yet. So they began there.

## The Door Is Not the End

The exact discharge date was missing. That annoyed Sam more than it annoyed Jo.

“It has to be somewhere.”

“Maybe.”

“No, it *has* to be somewhere.”

“In the world, yes.” He looked at her.

“That is not what I meant.”

The public material gave them a boundary instead. The patient had physically left before the DaTscan on 13 July. The claimed Protest had been dated to 10 or 11 July. So discharge had to sit somewhere between them. Not a date. A bracket. Sam wrote:

*after 10–11 JUL*

then, farther along:

*before 13 JUL*

Between the two he drew a short line.

“That’s all we get?”

“For now.” He looked dissatisfied. Jo looked at the line.

“It’s enough to stop us getting it wrong.”

That quieted him. They turned back to the chronology. The patient leaves. The specialist account does not. That was the next thing they had not expected. Sam said, “I keep thinking discharge is the end.”

“It is an end.”

“Not *the* end.”

“No.”

The physical episode closed before the documentary one did. On 13 July the DaTscan was performed. It was abnormal. Later that same day, at 21:41, the result was added to Document 04. Sam read the timestamp twice.

“After he’s gone,” Sam said. “And it still changes.”

He drew another line beneath the first and wrote: *body out; document open.* He looked at it.

“That’s strange.”

Jo nodded. Not wrong. Not suspicious. Just strange enough to matter. A person could be absent while the account of them was still acquiring content. A fact could arrive after the patient had left and still enter the document that future clinicians would inherit. The table suddenly had another kind of before and after. Not merely before and after events. Before and after **portability**. On 17 July, according to the governed record, final consultant sign-off followed. Sam tapped the date.

“So now?”

“Now what?”

“Now it’s finished.” Jo looked at the source note.

“As far as the chronology lets us say, yes. Final.”

“And portable.” Jo nodded.

He turned the completed synthesis over again. This time he did not read it as though it were simply another note. It had become something else. Not just a record of an encounter. A travelling thing. A compact account that could go where the patient went, and where he did not. A GP could read it. Another neurologist could read it. Podiatry could inherit parts of it. Years later, someone could meet the patient without ever meeting the admission. Sam said, “This is where the document starts doing work.” Jo looked at him.

“What kind?” He thought.

“Not just storing,” Sam said. “Carrying. Maybe even deciding.” Jo raised an eyebrow, and he corrected himself. “Influencing.”

“Better.” He wrote:

*not just record — working background*

Then they left it alone. The next material belonged to September. By 12 September, Professor Daniel Healy had reviewed the case. The letter said the patient probably had Park II. PARK2 testing was recommended first. There had also been dramatic motor improvement on procyclidine and rasagiline — two drugs used here as part of the neurological treatment strategy, now important because the patient’s movement state had visibly changed while taking them. Sam sat back.

“That’s a lot of new information.”

“It is.”

“None of it existed at discharge.”

“Correct.” He pointed to the phrase *dramatic improvement*.

“So this is a reopening.”

Jo did not answer immediately. They had agreed not to let old words arrive too easily. “A reason to ask again,” she said.

“That’s longer.”

“It’s safer.”

“Fine.” He wrote:

*new reason to ask again*

On 14 September, Podiatry saw the patient. Callus at the left fifth MTPJ. Toe props. Tight gastroc/soleus. Mechanical findings. Real findings. Sam smiled grimly.

“Here we go again.”

“Again what?”

“Both.”

Jo nodded. By then they had learned enough not to let the new neurological treatment response erase the structural feet. Nor would the structural findings erase the treatment response. The temptation kept coming back in different clothes. One explanation arrived with tablets. One arrived with callus and pressure. Both had evidence. Neither had earned the whole patient. Sam said, “This case refuses to be one thing.” Jo said, “Maybe that’s the point.” They read on. The next important event was harder because it had happened earlier than the document that preserved it. Sometime in September or October 2017, procyclidine was stopped for about two weeks because of a visual issue. The exact dates were not established. The later April 2018 note recorded what happened. The first thing that changed rapidly was the outside-left-foot problem.

The patient became more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.” Then procyclidine was restarted. Sam frowned at the page.

“So the event is in 2017, but the surviving record of it comes in 2018. Another split.” Jo nodded.

*event time*

and

*record time*

were no longer the same thing either. Sam drew them as two rows. He hated the page when he finished.

“It looks like railway timetables.”

“It is a little.”

“Except trains are less argumentative.”

The important part was narrower than either of them first wanted to make it. A neurological medication state changed. A foot phenomenon in the disputed territory changed rapidly with it. That did not mean the corn itself had become neurological. It did not mean every foot pain had been dystonia. It did not erase the mechanical Podiatry findings. But something at the outside left foot was now demonstrably sensitive to neurological treatment state. Sam said, “That difference tells you what to do.” Jo looked at him. There it was. An old sentence. Not quoted from anywhere on the table. Just available.

“What?”

“You heard yourself.” He had.

“That difference tells you what to do.” He glanced at the Goldmine file on the laptop without opening it.

“Maybe.” Jo smiled.

“Don’t classify it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking loudly.”

They kept moving. By 24 April 2018, the treating record explicitly contained the withdrawal/rechallenge history and the PARK2 suspicion. Sam put the July synthesis beside the April 2018 note. The two documents seemed to be looking at each other across nine months. One carried a possible separation. The other carried treatment-state evidence crossing the disputed territory. Sam said, “If you had only this later note, would you go back?”

“Go back where?”

“To the original account.” Jo looked at the July document.

“That depends what question you think is still open.”

He nodded slowly. That had become the rule beneath all the others. Not: What new fact arrived? But:

*What unresolved question does the new fact bear on?*

They left that sentence unwritten. It was doing enough already. The Podiatry pathway continued through at least March 2019. The detailed final Podiatry chronology was incomplete in the scoped material, but there was enough to know the structural stream did not simply vanish after the first visit. The world was not dividing itself into good evidence and bad. It was giving them different kinds of evidence about the same body. By 8 October 2020, Neurology recorded something new. A left foot “throw.” It looked somewhat like foot drop. But power was normal. The neurologist called it “actually parkinsonian.” Sam put his finger on the line.

“That’s strong,” Sam said. “Same territory, but a new classification. Does that change the old one?” Jo smiled.

“You’ve learned the right question.”

He was annoyed by how pleased that made him. The answer was not automatic. This later observation did not tell them what every painful foot phenomenon in 2017 had been. But it did something the old model had to live with. The foot territory could no longer be treated as neurologically silent. At least one conspicuous movement there had now been explicitly interpreted by treating Neurology as Parkinsonian. Sam turned back to Document 04. The phrase *may have been completely separate* looked different now. Not false. Not disproved. Just older. And carrying more weight than it had when it was first written. He said, “Is that possible?”

“What?”

“That a sentence can get heavier without changing.” Jo looked at the page.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“Because more people build on it.”

He thought about that. The sentence itself stayed still. Its consequences did not. That was enough to stop them for a while. Outside the kitchen window, rain had started. Not dramatic rain. The small persistent kind that makes a garden look busier than it is. Sam got up to close the back door. When he came back, Jo had put three documents in a row. July 2017. April 2018. October 2020. He stood behind her chair.

“What are you doing?”

“Looking for something.”

“What?”

“I’m not sure.”

They read them in order. The first document made room for separation. The second recorded neurological treatment-state change in the disputed foot territory. The third explicitly classified a foot-throw phenomenon as Parkinsonian. Sam said, “So the relationship is changing.” Jo shook her head.

“The evidence about the relationship is changing.” He sat down again.

“That’s annoyingly precise.”

“It matters.”

Because a model could survive new evidence in at least two ways. It could change. Or it could absorb the new fact without changing very much at all. They did not yet have the language for that. Or rather, they did, somewhere. But they had promised not to use it until they needed it. By September 2021, a small dose of Sinemet — levodopa combined with carbidopa, intended to improve dopamine-responsive movement symptoms — had begun. On 27 October, Neurology reviewed the transition. By 2022, the record said the feet took twenty to twenty-five minutes to “warm up” most mornings. Sam looked at the phrase.

“That’s odd.”

“What is?”

“Feet warming up.”

“Why?”

“Because it sounds temporal.”

Jo nodded. A structural lesion could hurt. A movement state could fluctuate. A medication state could change over time. A morning pattern was not a diagnosis. But it was a pattern. Something was happening not just in the foot, but across time. Sam said, “From here.” Jo looked at him.

“What?”

“From here.” He pointed to the morning note.

“If the question is what changes with time or medication, this is a way in.”

Jo smiled. Another old phrase had arrived without invitation. This time neither of them mentioned it. Then came March 2023. The table changed again. 26 March, 20:36. Urgent GP referral. Severe bilateral “burning” in the feet and lower legs. Extreme pain.

“Death wish.”

The GP was worried. Urgent Neurology review was sought. Jo stopped reading. Sam did not say anything for a while. The phrase changed the temperature of the room. No longer a technical problem about whether one symptom belonged under one explanation or another. No longer merely a question of provenance. The pain had become a safety problem. Sam read the note again, more slowly.

“So this is not just the same question louder,” Sam said. “It is another question.”

The mechanism still mattered. But now there was also: What happens when pain becomes this severe? What response is required when someone is saying life has become this difficult? What does the system owe to the present state, regardless of what happened in 2017? Jo wrote one word in the margin: *safety* Sam nodded. Two days later, at 12:48 on 28 March, a Movement Disorders nurse emailed the consultant. Ongoing “? neuropathic pain.” Pregabalin 600 mg per day. Debilitating pain. No quality of life. The patient said he had been off Parkinson medication for ten days without perceiving a difference from a Parkinson’s perspective. Sam read the lines.

“Now this looks like support for neuropathy.”

“Maybe.”

“And against Parkinson’s.”

“Maybe.” He looked at her.

“You’ve become unbearable.”

“Read the source-status problem.”

He did. The patient’s own interpretation of the medication withdrawal was important. It was not the same thing as an independent mechanistic finding. At the same time, later 2023 material contained possible OFF dystonia: abnormal posturing or cramping appearing when dopaminergic medication effect was low or wearing off. Stalevo associations — Stalevo being another levodopa-based Parkinson’s treatment, formulated to extend the useful effect of a dose. Leg pain and shuffling interpreted as OFF-related. A “misalignment” between clinician and patient. Fabry testing. A repeat DaTscan attempt. Sam spread the pages wider.

“This is messy.”

“Yes.”

“Good messy or bad messy?”

“Real messy.”

That was worse, and better. Because now the record contained several active explanatory possibilities at once. Neuropathic pain. OFF-related pain. Dystonia. Structural foot pathology. Medication-state effects. Walking burden. Pain modulation. None of them entirely displaced the others. Sam spread the papers wider. “The system is moving — quite a lot. So we cannot say nobody reconsidered anything. The harder question is whether all those local changes ever rebuilt the old relationship.”

Jo nodded. There. They had finally reached it without being told. The distinction had grown out of the record instead of being brought to it. Sam drew two columns: *local change* and *whole model*. Under the first he listed OFF dystonia, Stalevo timing, pregabalin, Podiatry findings, the Parkinsonian foot throw and pain modulation. Under the second he wrote nothing. Not because nothing had happened. Because he did not yet know what would count. Jo looked at the blank column.

“That’s the harder one.”

“What would go there?”

“A new account of how the pieces fit.” He looked at the earlier pages.

“Not just another label.”

“No.”

“Not just another treatment.”

“No.”

“Not just another specialist.”

“No.”

“Then what?” Jo tapped the blank column.

“Something that changes what the old facts mean together.”

Sam sat back. The rain had stopped. For a few minutes the kitchen was very quiet. Then he said:

“One whole.”

Jo looked at him. This time the old language did not feel imported. It felt earned. They left it there. Not as an answer. As a possibility.

## What Would Make It Let Go?

The blank column stayed on the table overnight. Sam had drawn a box around the heading *whole model* before they stopped. Nothing beneath it. The next morning Jo found the page under a cereal bowl.

“Very archival.”

“It was dry.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is for cereal.”

They put the page back between them. By 2024 the record contained another useful split. Pregabalin, a drug commonly used for neuropathic pain, had helped the pain. Toe curling remained. Sam read the note and nodded.

“That’s helpful.”

“How?”

“One thing changes. Another doesn’t.” Jo looked at him.

“So?”

“So we don’t have to pretend they were one thing.”

She smiled. Now the distinction felt ordinary. Pain could improve. Toe curling could remain. The partial response did not have to vindicate a total theory. It separated what had been travelling together. Sam wrote: *pain better; curling still there.* Then, underneath: *not the same after all.* Jo glanced at him.

“You’re doing it again.”

“What?”

“That sentence.”

He looked down. The old language was becoming hard to distinguish from ordinary speech. That was probably a good sign. In the same period, alcohol-related neuropathy remained a concern in the record. The patient later said he stopped alcohol completely on 7 August 2024. The concern did not instantly disappear from the documents. Sam frowned.

“Should it?”

“I don’t know.”

“If alcohol stops, the explanation should weaken.”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe?”

“What if it’s about historical damage?” He sat back.

“Right.”

They had learned by then that a fact losing one support did not automatically make the whole explanation collapse. That would be too easy. The better question was becoming:

*What would count as enough to reduce its authority?*

Sam wrote it down and underlined *enough*. Jo looked at the word.

“That one again.”

He nodded. By the 2025 clinic, the morning pain had become severe. The first steps were described as torture. The pain improved once Parkinson medication reached the system. Slow-release Sinemet was considered overnight. On 10 March, according to the later clinician account, Sinemet CR was prescribed. Then came 19 March. The complaint. Sam had been expecting it by then. Even so, reading it changed the work. Up to this point they had been reading a clinical record. Now they were reading somebody trying to reconstruct what had happened to him. Jo noticed it first.

“The voice changes.”

Sam nodded. The patient was no longer only inside the record as someone being observed. He was looking back at it. Trying to work out what had been done with the things he had said. Trying to understand why the feet had remained unresolved for so long. Trying to place the recent relief from Sinemet CR into a history that had previously made sense to him in another way. Sam said, “So this is where he gets the whole picture.”

“No.”

“He’s complaining.”

“Yes.”

“So he must have all the records.”

“No.”

She pointed to the complaint. He had not yet obtained all the internal notes, referrals and correspondence. Sam read that part again.

“So he’s challenging before full access. On what basis?”

“His body, his memory, what he has been told, and what has just happened with the medication.” Sam looked at the page for a long time.

“That’s awkward.”

“For whom?”

“Everyone.”

The recent response to Sinemet CR had given the patient something direct. Not an archive. Not a theory. A felt change. The feet that had become intolerable were now profoundly relieved. That did not prove a complete mechanism. But it changed what the patient could no longer ignore. Sam said, “The body gets there before the file does.” Jo looked at him.

“That’s good.” He shrugged.

“It’s just what happened.”

They put that beside the earlier timeline. The patient had spent years living inside an account before he could inspect how the account had been made. That asymmetry became obvious only after the complaint. The institution had the documents and the history it had written. The patient had the pain and the history he remembered. Neither side possessed everything. Then, after the complaint, the documentary world began to open. The clinician response came on 25 March. It acknowledged that Sinemet was associated with pain relief, but framed the change as an alteration of the pain experience “of whatever cause.” Sam read the phrase twice.

“That is clever.” Jo looked at him.

“Clever?”

“It keeps the response without letting the response decide the cause.”

“Yes.”

“Is that wrong?”

“No.” He waited.

“That’s irritating.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted one of these things to be wrong.” Jo smiled.

“That would make it easier.”

The phrase was careful. It allowed the treatment effect in. It did not surrender the older uncertainty. By then they knew enough to notice what the phrase accomplished. A model could become more flexible. More accommodating. More capable of taking in apparently contrary information. That might be a sign of strength. Or it might be the way the model avoided ever having to change. Sam wrote: *flexible is not the same as corrected.* Jo added: *or the same as wrong.* They both looked at the page. “Annoying,” Sam said.

“Useful.”

By 15 April, the complaint had been treated as a matter of clinical judgment and routed accordingly. By 30 May it had entered a formal governance pathway. A PAR. Then SIMT. The acronyms made Sam visibly tired.

“I preferred feet.”

“They’re still here.”

“They’re buried under administration.”

That was partly the point. Once the complaint entered institutional review, the story accumulated another layer of representation. The clinical record existed. Then the complaint about the clinical record. Then the response to the complaint. Then the review of the complaint. Then the outcome of the review. Each layer described what came before it. Each layer could become another thing future readers inherited. Sam drew a stack: event, record, complaint, response, review, outcome. Underneath he added: *later reader.* Jo looked at it.

“What does the later reader get?”

“The stack.”

“Do they get the event?” He stared at the first word.

“Not directly.”

They had arrived at another problem without meaning to. The world did not merely contain facts. It contained representations of representations. And every time something moved one layer farther away from the original event, source status mattered more. The patient then made rectification and access requests. The language became legal and procedural. Article 16. Article 15. Article 18. Jo did not let Sam skip them.

“They matter.”

“They are very unromantic.”

“This is not a romance.”

“I had noticed.”

The requests were attempts to do something specific. Correct. Inspect. Restrict. Trace. Find out what had been said. Find out what had been kept. Find out what had travelled. On 7 July 2025, an Article 15 request explicitly sought records or traces of the alleged 10–11 July 2017 bedside interaction. For the first time in the retained core they were using, the Protest was pinned to approximate dates and alleged participants. Sam tapped the page.

“So now the event is documented.”

“The claim is documented.” He exhaled.

“Yes.”

They had become almost tedious about that distinction, which was how they knew they meant to keep it. On 5 August the hospital responded that it did not hold certain categories of student, ward-round or teaching records responsive to the request. Sam leaned back.

“So no evidence.”

“No responsive records held in those categories.”

“That’s what I said.”

“No.” He stared at her. She waited. Eventually he sighed.

“Fine. We cannot turn ‘nothing found there’ into ‘didn’t happen.’”

“Correct.”

“But we also can’t turn it into ‘did happen.’”

“Correct.” He rubbed his face.

“This entire project is a machine for stopping sentences just before they become satisfying.” Jo laughed.

“That may be the most accurate thing you’ve said.”

On 11 July, four days after the Article 15 request, the GP sent an urgent referral to St Vincent’s for a second opinion. The referral carried the established young-onset Parkinson diagnosis. It also carried the patient’s challenge and request for movement-disorders and dystonia expertise. Sam read it slowly.

“So the old account travels into the place that is supposed to reassess it,” Sam said. “It has to carry some history, but the history is part of what is being challenged. Can it do that fairly?” He looked at the referral again.

“That’s difficult.”

It was. An external service needed enough inherited information to understand the case. But inherited information could also shape what the external service saw. Too little continuity and the patient became a blank slate. Too much and the new review risked beginning inside the very model it was meant to test. Sam wrote: *reopening needs history; history can constrain reopening.* Jo added a line between them. “Held in counterbalance,” Sam said. She smiled. He looked pleased. This time the phrase landed cleanly. Not as decoration. As the thing itself. By 27 August, the PAR had reached SIMT. On 3 September, the outcome letter said the care decisions were appropriate. No deficit. Sinemet first prescribed in 2021. The institutional conclusion was now portable too. Sam put the letter on top of the stack he had drawn.

“Now this becomes another authority.”

“Yes.”

“Does it settle it?”

“For whom?” He looked at her.

“Good question.”

The institution had reached a conclusion. That fact was real. The full source-by-source evidence pack used by the review was not visible in the scoped material. That fact was also real. Sam said, “So I can say the institution decided. I cannot say that decision, by itself, proves the underlying history was reconstructed properly — and I cannot say the opposite merely because the full pack is missing.”

“Exactly.”

He stared at the outcome letter. Then wrote: *conclusion is not method.* Jo nodded. They had become good at these. That worried Sam slightly.

“I don’t want this to turn into a collection of little distinctions.”

“It won’t.”

“How do you know?”

“Because the distinctions are starting to point at the same problem.” He waited. She looked at the stack of documents.

“The question is becoming whether anything can make an inherited account stop governing.” Sam looked at her.

“Release.”

The word sat between them. Jo did not object. This time it was not premature. The old account did not need to be deleted. It did not need to be declared fraudulent. It did not even need to become wholly false. For something new to happen, perhaps the old account only had to lose the right to decide everything that came next. The old thing could remain. Its claim could disappear. Sam said, “That’s different from correction.”

“Yes.”

“Correction changes the account.”

“Maybe.”

“Release changes what the account is allowed to govern.”

Jo nodded. There it was. The shed vocabulary had reached the new world without making the new world smaller. They wrote nothing for a while. Then came December 2025. The patient later said he had received an authoritative diagnosis of dystonia on 19 December. QPS would separately acknowledge that a dystonia diagnosis had been made in December. The primary report itself was not in the scoped core. Sam looked at Jo.

“So we have the diagnosis.”

“We have later evidence that the diagnosis was given.”

“Not the report.”

“Correct.”

They both smiled. By then the qualification came automatically. In January 2026 the patient renewed his challenge. Later that month, QPS responded. The response was recalled and materially the same response was reissued. The visible institutional position was that the later dystonia diagnosis did not require reopening the prior governance conclusion. Sam sat very still. There was no need to dramatise it. The sentence needed no help.

“What would?” Jo looked at him.

“What would what?”

“What would make the old conclusion lose authority?”

Neither of them answered. They had been circling that question for hours. Now the record itself seemed to ask it. If new evidence could arrive and be accepted without changing the earlier conclusion, what kind of evidence would count? A later dystonia diagnosis? Not necessarily. A medication response? Not necessarily. A Parkinsonian foot observation? Not necessarily. OFF dystonia? Not necessarily. Structural findings? Those were already there. The answer could not simply be “more evidence.” Sam said, “Maybe the problem isn’t whether new facts arrive.” Jo nodded.

“It’s whether they can change the status of old facts.”

He looked at the stack. That was closer. Then February 2026 arrived. A St Vincent’s letter, dictated on 3 February and signed on 4 February, recorded two pathogenic Parkin variants. The letter attributed Parkinsonism and dystonia to compound-heterozygous Parkin mutations. Sam put the page down.

“There.” Jo did not respond.

“There what?”

“The answer.”

“To which question?” He laughed.

“You really have ruined me.”

She waited. He looked again. The later genetics clarified the later biological picture. Parkinsonism and dystonia were now linked through the Parkin result. But the genetics could not travel backwards and become something clinicians knew in July 2017. Nor could it establish, by itself, what every painful foot phenomenon had been. Nor could it tell them whether the founding specialist account had been reasonable when made. The later result was powerful. It was not a time machine. Sam said, “So even the biggest fact has limits. That’s almost rude.”

Jo smiled. They placed the genetic letter at the far right of the timeline. The table was now full. GP notes. Referral. Neurology. Podiatry. Document 04. The Protest claim. Medication events. Later neurological observations. Pain crisis. Complaint. Responses. Review. External referral. Dystonia. Genetics. For a while neither of them touched anything. Then Sam said, “I think I know what this world is.” Jo looked at him.

“Go on.”

“It is not mainly a world where facts are missing,” Sam said. “Sometimes they are, but that is not the centre of it. It is a world where facts have different jobs. Some start questions; some answer them; some are inherited; some only look independent because they descend from an older proposition. Some change treatment, some change the model, and some do neither.” He looked at the whole table. “And sometimes a fact clearly matters, but the real question is whether it is allowed to matter enough.”

Jo leaned back. That was new. Not from the shed. Not from the record’s terminology exactly. Not from one document. It had emerged from the crossing of both worlds. Sam saw her expression.

“What?”

“Say that again.” He thought.

“Some evidence matters.”

“No.”

“The question is whether it matters enough?”

“Closer.”

He looked at the old July document. Then the later ones. Finally he said:

“Whether it can change what already counts as settled.”

Jo nodded. There. The room became quiet again. Not because they had solved the history. Because they had finally found the shape of the problem they were actually studying. Sam picked up the July synthesis. Then the February 2026 letter. He held one in each hand.

“Same patient,” Sam said, holding one document in each hand. “Different information state. Different authority. Same unresolved history.” Jo looked at the table.

“Maybe.” He waited. She pointed to the later letter.

“This resolves some things.” Then to the July document.

“This settled some things.” Then to the whole line between them.

“And those aren’t the same.” Sam slowly put both pages down.

“Settlement isn’t truth.”

“No.”

“It’s when the live demand closes.” Jo smiled.

“You remember.”

“I remember the shed.”

“So do I.” He looked at the timeline.

“Then this is worse.”

“What is?”

“In the shed, if something was settled too early, we could just loosen it again.” Jo laughed.

“You’re suggesting we apply a four-millimetre hex key to clinical governance?”

“I’m suggesting we keep our options open.” He got up to make tea. At the kettle he turned.

“One more thing.”

“What?”

“The old document never has to disappear, does it? It can stay there and still stop deciding everything.”

Jo looked at the finished July synthesis. “Yes.” Sam nodded.

“Release.” This time neither of them needed to explain it.

## The Words Come Back

The next morning Sam arrived with the shed notebook under his arm. Jo saw it before he had taken off his coat.

“No.”

“I haven’t opened it.”

“You brought it.”

“I thought that was the honest version.”

He put it on the table. The cover had a bent corner and a mug ring near the bottom. For two days they had managed without it. That mattered. The record had already supplied its own language: information state, provenance, Podiatry, neuropathy, dystonia, OFF, complaint, review, genetics. More importantly, it had supplied its own difficulties. An event was not the record of the event. Repetition was not independent corroboration. Structural pathology was not structural independence. A later result was not earlier knowledge. Local adjustment was not reconstruction of the whole. Jo touched the notebook.

“What do you want it to do?”

“Not explain the case.”

“Good.”

“Help us say what kind of things keep happening.”

“That it might do.”

He opened it. The old words were still there: Differentiation, Opening, Relating, Repair, Integration, Settlement. Underneath, Focal, Distributed, Release. Then Standing, Bearing, Efficacy. Sam looked from the page to the records.

“They look small now.”

“They were always small.”

“In the shed, the things were small too.”

“That helped.”

He started with Differentiation. The Article 15 response was an easy temptation. No responsive teaching or ward-round records had been found in the specified categories. Sam had first read that as evidence that the alleged bedside event had not happened. Now he did not. “What was the question?” Jo asked.

“Whether the search result established non-occurrence.”

“And?”

“It established the search result.”

She smiled. That was enough. The difference mattered because it changed what they were entitled to infer. Whether that persistence was best called Standing or Efficacy could wait. The useful part had already happened. They tried the 2020 foot observation. A movement that looked like foot drop had been examined in the presence of normal power and described as actually Parkinsonian. “Differentiation,” Sam said.

“Maybe. Between what?”

“Appearance and mechanism.”

“Too broad.” He looked again.

“Between weakness-based foot drop and a Parkinsonian movement that resembles it.”

“Better.”

“Focal. Bearing.” Jo shook her head.

“What?”

“You’re decorating it before you’ve said what completed.” He sighed, then tried again.

“The movement that looked like one thing became intelligible as another because preserved power changed what the appearance meant.”

Jo left that alone. The exercise was slower than he remembered, which reassured him. If the vocabulary fitted instantly, it was probably doing too much of the work. They moved to the July synthesis. “Settlement,” Sam said. Jo waited.

“The document settles the case.”

“No.”

“Parts of it.”

“Which parts?”

He read rather than answered from memory. Parkinsonism had become sufficient for treatment planning. Podiatry had become an operative destination. A treatment strategy existed. The relation between the painful feet and the neurological state remained less clear.

“So something can be settled enough to proceed without being resolved in every direction.”

“Of course.” He wrote two lines:

*resolved*

*settled enough to proceed*

“That second one is dangerous,” he said.

“Not always.”

He crossed out *dangerous* and wrote *powerful*. That was closer. Action did not require certainty without remainder. It required enough for the next legitimate step. The harder question was: enough for which step? If the live demand was *Do we have enough to treat the Parkinsonism?*, one answer might be yes. If it was *Do we understand how the painful feet relate to the gait and lower-limb motor state?*, the answer was different. “Same document,” Sam said.

“Different unfinished business.”

That one they knew. Same carrier. Different completion. Podiatry made the point again. Treating a callus did not make the episode Repair by definition. Identifying the exact mechanical site might be Differentiation. Establishing how loading and posture bore on each other might be Relating. Restoring a painful local lesion to an operative condition might be Repair. Deciding there was a legitimate structural treatment target might be Settlement. Same foot. Same service. Different question. “This is harder than the shed,” Sam said. Jo looked at the notebook.

“It’s harder to cheat.”

He laughed. The 2017 procyclidine withdrawal gave them another trap. “It opens the old question again,” Sam said.

“For us.”

He stopped. A later reader could experience an Opening because a piece of evidence restored access to a question. That did not make the historical event itself an Opening. The coordinate belonged to the completed intelligibility, not to the excitement of the person finding it years later. Sam closed his pen.

“I remember this being easier.”

“You had screws.”

They looked at the chronology as a whole. Referral. Admission. Synthesis. Treatment-state changes. Podiatry. The 2020 foot movement. The 2023 crisis. Complaint. Review. External referral. Genetics. “Relating,” Sam said. Jo gave him a look. “The hard part is the arrows,” he said. That was better. Feet to gait. Neurological state to loading. Loading to pain. Medication to movement. Movement to foot behaviour. Document to later interpretation. Earlier proposition to later repetition. Later evidence back toward the inherited model. Sam looked at the papers.

“Who owned the arrows?”

Different services could own different objects. Podiatry could own a callus. Neurology could own Parkinsonism. Primary care could own continuing symptoms. Governance could own a complaint. Relationships were easier to lose. “Boxes have departments,” Sam said.

“Arrows don’t.”

He wrote that one down. Integration was the old word that now fitted most naturally. Not because integration sounded virtuous. Not because everything should be made into one cause. The record already showed why that would be a mistake. Integration mattered because separate, valid pieces could acquire roles in one larger account without ceasing to be separate pieces. Structural pathology. Dystonic-looking movement. Medication-state change. Parkinsonian foot movement. OFF pain. Pregabalin response. Dopaminergic pain response. A pile of truths was not yet a whole. Sam found the blank column from the day before.

*whole model*

Under it he wrote:

*roles*

*arrows*

*what changes what*

Then, after a pause:

*what would force revision*

Jo pointed to the last line.

“That’s not Integration.”

“What is it?”

“Something this place has that the shed didn’t make us name.”

“Corrigibility.”

They had seen the word before. Now it belonged to them. Not whether a model could add information. Not whether it could become more elaborate. Whether contrary evidence could actually reduce its authority. Sam wrote beneath it:

*What would make this stop governing?*

Then he looked at Focal, Distributed, Release. “Release,” he said. Jo waited this time.

“The old account can stay,” Sam said. “The observations and the reasoning can stay; parts of it can remain right. Release would mean that later evidence has become sufficient for it to stop deciding what happens next.”

Jo nodded. That was Release. Not deletion. Not necessarily correction. De-governance. The old thing could remain; its claim on the result could disappear. They tried the neighbours. Correct a mistaken phrase in the record: perhaps Repair. Leave the phrase untouched but cease treating it as governing: perhaps Release. Rebuild the longitudinal account so structural and neurological findings acquire determinate roles in one coherent arrangement: Integration. Bring a live question to sufficient closure for action: Settlement. The distinctions mattered because the record contained the possibilities without guaranteeing the achievements. “We’re not classifying the case,” Jo said.

“No.”

“We’re asking what would count as completion for each live demand.”

The case was not one completion. It was many: local, distributed, achieved, claimed, absent, still live. That changed the scale without changing the grammar. In the shed, a distributed completion might take three brackets and a length of timber. Here it could require observations separated by years, several services and documents with different evidential status. “Distributed feels bigger,” Sam said.

“Longer.”

He nodded. One note could establish a serious current safety signal. No single note could reconstruct an eight-year relationship among pain, movement, structure, medication and inherited explanation. Same history. Different question. Different topology. Sam closed the notebook.

“Done?”

“We haven’t touched persistence.”

He opened it again. Standing. Bearing. Efficacy. The 2020 foot classification was enough to show why they had to be careful. If the completed matter was simply that the movement had been identified as Parkinsonian rather than weakness-based foot drop, the achieved determination itself remained: Standing. If the completion lay in how the appearance was understood through its relation to preserved power, Bearing did more work. If the live question concerned what the classification enabled clinicians to do next, Efficacy might be the relevant form. Sam looked at the three words.

“So it still comes back to the question.”

“Always.” He wrote across the top of the page:

**FREEZE THE UNRESOLVED QUESTION BEFORE CLASSIFYING THE COMPLETION**

The sentence looked sterner here than it had in the shed. Here, getting the question wrong could make a description of intelligibility look like a verdict on care, motive or causation. The grammar could not do that work. It could not establish the Protest. It could not decide negligence. It could not turn an inference into a fact. It could not replace an expert conclusion. It was a way of seeing completions, not a judge. That boundary made the notebook lighter. They returned to the complaint. “What does it complete?” Sam asked.

“Freeze it.”

He did. For the patient, the unresolved demand was no longer only clinical. He wanted the historical account examined. He wanted records, explanation, preservation and independent review. The complaint did not satisfy those demands. It made them institutionally live.

“Opening,” he said. “And maybe Efficacy. It changes what the institution now has to do.” Jo considered it.

“That sounds earned.”

Different world. Same form. Then the SIMT outcome. If the live question was whether the institution would conclude there had been a deficit in care, the outcome settled that institutional question. If the question was whether the source record had been independently reconstructed in a way an outside reader could inspect, the letter did not settle it; the full evidential pack was not available to them. Sam looked up.

“This is where arguments go wrong.”

“How?”

“One document closes one question and people let the closure spread.” Jo smiled.

“Settlement leaks.”

He wrote it in the margin. A referral could settle where someone went without settling why the problem existed. A diagnosis could settle the presence of a syndrome without settling every symptom relation. A treatment response could settle that something changed without settling why. A governance outcome could settle an institutional position without settling the adequacy of its reconstruction for an outside reader. Genetics could settle a later molecular relationship without settling what was knowable nine years earlier. One closed demand did not close the ones beside it. “That feels new,” Sam said.

“It is.”

“What do we call it?”

Jo thought about the circle he had drawn around one answer and the larger circle around the questions beside it.

“Maybe every completion has a boundary.”

“A jurisdiction?” She made a face.

“Too legal?”

“Useful, though.”

He wrote it beside Corrigibility. Two words the shed had not supplied. The new world had not broken the grammar. It had made the grammar answer for itself.

## What the Answer Is Allowed to Answer

They left *jurisdiction* on the page while they made lunch. When they came back it still looked too legal. Sam circled it anyway. The idea underneath it was ordinary enough. An answer could be good and still answer only one question. The July synthesis showed them how easily that boundary could disappear. Parkinsonism could become sufficient for treatment planning without settling the feet–gait relationship. Podiatry could be an appropriate destination without settling neurological contribution to loading. A later medication response could establish change with treatment state without identifying one historical cause. A governance review could establish the institution’s conclusion without, by itself, making its evidential method visible. Nothing false had to be said. The dangerous step was quieter:

*enough for this*

becoming *enough for that too*. Sam tapped the two lines.

“Completion has a boundary.”

“That’s less legal.”

“Keep jurisdiction?”

“For when we need the sharper word.”

The complaint response gave them the next problem. The clinician had acknowledged Sinemet-associated pain relief while leaving the cause open: pain relief “of whatever cause.” The formulation could take in the treatment effect without surrendering the older uncertainty. Sam had called that clever the day before. Now he tried again.

“Adaptable.”

“Locally adaptable,” Jo said. Treatment changed. New possibilities appeared. Symptoms were reconsidered. The record was plainly not frozen. But local adaptability did not tell them whether the organising relation had been rebuilt. Sam looked at the page headed *whole model*.

“So a model can change a lot and keep the same shape.”

“That I believe.”

They returned to Corrigibility. The question was not whether new facts could be added. Plainly they could. It was whether any observation could make the inherited arrangement lose authority. “The exit condition,” Sam said. Every working model needed one somewhere: some imaginable evidence that would make it stop being the model. Without that, survival meant very little. A model could survive because it had been tested and retained. Or because every contrary fact could be given a harmless place inside it. “How do we tell?” Sam asked. Jo did not answer. They went back through the later record instead. The outside-left-foot change during procyclidine withdrawal. The 2020 Parkinsonian foot movement. OFF-related pain and shuffling. Possible OFF dystonia. The morning warm-up. Sinemet-related pain relief. The later dystonia diagnosis. The PRKN result.

Every one could be fitted somewhere. That fact alone did not show whether the old arrangement had passed the test. Only that it had survived. Sam wrote two lines:

*robust under testing*

*resistant to losing*

From the outside, they could look identical. In the shed, a shelf either held or it did not. Here, even what counted as a test could be shaped by the account being tested. Sam turned back to the stack.

“If the first account gives later people the categories, later notes are partly written inside those categories.”

“Yes.”

“So later agreement may not be independent agreement.”

Now Jo reached for the pencil. A founding account could influence what was noticed, what was referred, what language was used, what treatment was tried, even what the patient later came to call the problem. The record could grow thicker in the vocabulary it had already supplied. Then the thickness itself could look like confirmation.

“Dependency graph,” Sam said. “Not stack.”

That had earned its place. Ten later notes might contain ten independent observations. Or several might descend from one earlier formulation. Number alone could not tell them. Provenance mattered because agreement had genealogy. “Relating,” Jo said. Sam smiled.

“You said it first.” She ignored him and wrote three questions:

*Where did this come from?*

*What does it depend on?*

*Another root, or another branch?*

By late afternoon the table held fewer papers than it had in the morning. Sam had started filing documents once their particular job was done. “You’re tidying,” Jo said.

“I’m integrating.”

“You’re putting paper in a folder.”

“That too.”

The work had begun with accumulation. Competence now looked more like subtraction: knowing which piece answered which question, what had to stay live, what could stop governing. They returned to *Not clear*. Sam had once read the phrase as weakness. Now he was less sure.

“Maybe it was the honest answer.”

“It might have been.”

“So uncertainty isn’t the problem.”

“No.” Jo set the phrase beside the Podiatry plan.

“Uncertainty can still organise action.”

Sam saw it. A record could say *Not clear* while care nevertheless proceeded through separated pathways. That was not automatically wrong. But it was a stronger practical state than the words alone suggested. “Epistemically uncertain,” he said.

“Operationally committed.”

The phrase finally had somewhere to land. Unresolved did not mean inactive. An unresolved relation could still route care, divide ownership and shape what later observations were allowed to mean. The claimed Protest tested the same discipline from another direction. Reported retrospectively. Not contemporaneously corroborated in the retained core. Later absence of responsive records not proof of non-occurrence. “What does the grammar do with a disputed event?” Sam asked.

“Nothing magical.”

That disappointed him. If the question was the evidential status of the Protest, its source status could become clear without the event itself becoming proved. If the question was what the Protest would mean *if it occurred*, they could reason conditionally: it would function as notice that the patient believed the original problem remained unresolved before documentary closure. They did not have to choose between promoting it to fact and pretending it had no analytical significance. “Held in counterbalance,” Sam said. Jo nodded. The phrase had become ordinary now. They turned to the genetics once more. The result was powerful enough to invite hindsight. Parkinsonism and dystonia were later linked through pathogenic Parkin variants. “Integration,” Sam said.

“Later.”

“I know.”

The later result could reorganise present retrospective understanding. It could not become evidence that clinicians possessed a molecular result in 2017, and it did not by itself identify the cause of every earlier painful foot phenomenon. Even the strongest fact on the table had a boundary. Sam looked at the spread of papers.

“Nothing gets to be everything.” Jo considered it.

“Keep that.”

He wrote it once, without a list underneath. Nothing gets to be everything. Not the diagnosis. Not the referral. Not the treatment response. Not the complaint, the review or the genetics. “And not the theory,” Jo said. Sam added that in smaller writing. That mattered most. The old vocabulary had survived because it had not been allowed to become sovereign. It could describe the form of a completion. It could not replace the clinical record, decide disputed history, establish motive, determine negligence or supply an expert conclusion. The grammar had a jurisdiction too. Sam closed the shed notebook. This time Jo did not object. He picked up his mug, looked inside and made a face.

“Cold.”

“Again?”

“Apparently.”

Jo gathered the last loose page into the folder. Sam carried both mugs to the sink. “Another?” he asked.

“Fresh one.” He filled the kettle. The notebook stayed closed.
